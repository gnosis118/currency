# How Much Foreign Currency Should You Take on Vacation? Complete 2025 Guide

**Quick Answer:** Plan to carry $50-100 per person per day in local currency for a budget trip, $150-250 for mid-range travel, or $300+ for luxury vacations. However, the optimal mix is 20-30% cash and 70-80% credit/debit cards to maximize savings and security. Actual amounts vary dramatically by destination—cash-dependent countries like Thailand need more physical currency, while card-friendly destinations like Sweden require minimal cash.

Standing at the airport exchange kiosk with your boarding pass in hand, you face a dilemma every international traveler encounters: How much foreign currency should I actually convert? Take too little and you'll scramble for ATMs in unfamiliar cities, potentially paying expensive fees. Take too much and you'll lose money converting unused currency back at terrible rates—or worse, have it stolen or lost.

The question of how much foreign money to take on vacation has no universal answer because every destination, travel style, and trip length requires different amounts. A backpacker exploring Southeast Asia needs far less daily cash than a family visiting Disneyland Paris. A business traveler in London primarily uses cards, while someone touring rural Morocco must carry substantial cash for most transactions.

This comprehensive guide eliminates guesswork by providing destination-specific recommendations, budget calculations for different travel styles, strategies for mixing cash and cards optimally, and critical safety measures to protect your money abroad. Whether you're planning a weekend getaway to Montreal or a three-week adventure through South America, you'll know exactly how much currency to take and in what form.

## Calculating Your Foreign Currency Needs

Before exchanging a single dollar, you need to determine your total cash requirement based on destination characteristics, trip length, and personal spending patterns.

### The $50-100 Per Day Rule (And When It Doesn't Apply)

Travel experts commonly recommend budgeting $50-100 per person daily for foreign currency needs. This rule provides a useful starting point but requires substantial adjustment based on specific circumstances.

**When $50-100 daily works well:**
- **Mid-range European travel**: Accommodation and transportation pre-booked, mixture of restaurant meals and supermarket food, some paid attractions
- **Budget Asian travel**: Hostel or budget hotel, street food and local restaurants, public transportation, free or low-cost activities
- **Short trips with advance planning**: Major expenses like hotels already paid, only daily spending money needed

**When you need significantly more ($200-400+ daily):**
- **Luxury travel**: High-end hotels, fine dining, private tours, spa treatments, premium transportation
- **Cash-dependent countries**: Rural areas with limited card acceptance, developing nations, destinations with unreliable electronic payment infrastructure
- **Expensive destinations**: Switzerland, Iceland, Norway, Japan (outside major cities), Australia, Scandinavia
- **Family travel**: Multiplied per-person costs, higher activity expenses, more meals at restaurants

**When you need less ($20-50 daily):**
- **Card-friendly destinations**: Sweden, Denmark, Netherlands, South Korea where cards accepted universally even for small purchases
- **All-inclusive resorts**: Accommodation, meals, drinks, activities included; cash only for tips and off-resort excursions
- **Business travel**: Company card covers most expenses, minimal personal spending needed

The reality is that calculating foreign currency needs requires moving beyond simple daily averages to understand what you'll actually spend money on during your specific trip.

### Breaking Down Daily Expenses by Category

Create accurate currency budgets by itemizing expenses rather than guessing at daily totals. Here's how to calculate category by category:

**Accommodation:**
Most travelers pre-book and pre-pay hotels through booking platforms, eliminating this from your cash budget. However, some situations require cash:
- Guesthouses in developing countries often charge in local currency on checkout
- Rural accommodations may not accept cards
- Some budget properties offer 5-10% discounts for cash payment
- City taxes and tourist fees sometimes must be paid in cash at checkout

**Food and beverage:**
Daily food costs vary enormously by destination and eating habits:
- **Budget** ($15-30/day): Street food, supermarkets, local restaurants, cooking some meals
- **Mid-range** ($40-80/day): Mix of local and international restaurants, cafes, occasional nice dinners
- **High-end** ($100-300/day): Fine dining, hotel restaurants, room service, premium cafes

Calculate meals separately: breakfast $5-15, lunch $10-25, dinner $15-50, plus snacks and drinks $10-20. Multiply by trip days and number of travelers.

**Transportation:**
Local transportation requirements depend heavily on destination:
- **Public transit-focused trips** ($5-15/day): Metro cards, buses, occasional taxis
- **Mixed transportation** ($20-40/day): Trains, Uber/taxis, some public transit
- **Car rental or frequent taxis** ($50-100/day): Private transportation, parking fees, tolls

Many public transit systems require cash for tickets, though major cities increasingly accept cards.

**Activities and attractions:**
Entry fees for museums, tours, attractions, and experiences often represent significant portions of travel budgets:
- **Budget travelers** ($10-30/day): Free activities, parks, self-guided walking tours, occasional museum
- **Mid-range travelers** ($40-80/day): Paid attractions, guided tours, activities, some experiences
- **Experience-focused travelers** ($100-200+/day): Premium tours, adventure activities, shows, special experiences

Research major attractions beforehand and factor actual costs into calculations. Booking tours online in advance often saves money and eliminates need to carry that cash.

**Miscellaneous expenses:**
Don't forget smaller categories that add up:
- **Tips and gratuities** ($5-20/day): Service staff, guides, drivers
- **Souvenirs and shopping** ($10-100/day depending on shopping plans)
- **Snacks, coffee, drinks** ($10-25/day)
- **Emergency buffer** (10-20% of total budget)

Once you've calculated each category, multiply by trip length and add 20% buffer for unexpected expenses. This gives you a realistic total cash need—but remember, you shouldn't carry all this as physical currency.

### Optimal Cash vs. Card Mix

The ideal strategy uses multiple payment methods to maximize security, minimize fees, and ensure you're never stranded without access to money:

**Recommended payment split:**
- **Credit cards (no foreign transaction fees)**: 60-70% of expenses
- **Debit card for ATM withdrawals**: 25-35% converted to local currency as needed
- **Pre-converted cash**: 5-10% for arrival buffer and emergency backup

This approach means if you calculate needing $2,000 for a week-long trip:
- Use credit card for $1,200-1,400 (hotels, restaurants, major purchases)
- Withdraw $500-700 from ATMs in destination (daily expenses, cash-only vendors)
- Carry $100-200 pre-converted (immediate needs upon arrival, emergency backup)

**Why this mix works:**
- **Security**: Stolen cards can be cancelled and replaced; stolen cash is gone forever
- **Better rates**: Credit cards typically offer excellent exchange rates; bank ATMs give good rates; airport exchanges charge terrible rates
- **Lower fees**: One $10 ATM fee on $400 withdrawal costs 2.5%; multiple $3 fees on $50 withdrawals cost 6% each
- **Flexibility**: Multiple payment methods mean you always have backup if one fails

## Destination-Specific Currency Requirements

How much cash you need varies dramatically by destination due to differences in card acceptance, ATM availability, and local payment customs.

### Cash-Friendly vs. Card-Friendly Countries

Understanding your destination's payment infrastructure helps determine how much physical currency to carry.

**Highly card-friendly (minimal cash needed: $100-300 for entire trip):**

**Scandinavia (Sweden, Norway, Denmark)**: These countries lead the world in cashless payments. Many merchants don't accept cash at all. Budget just $50-100 for emergencies; cards work everywhere from Stockholm's subway to rural Norwegian gas stations.

**Netherlands and Belgium**: Near-universal card acceptance including small purchases. Carry only $100-150 for street vendors and rare cash-only situations.

**South Korea**: Extensive card infrastructure even in small shops and taxis. Credit and debit cards work almost everywhere. Plan for $150-200 cash maximum.

**Australia**: Very high card penetration though small rural shops may prefer cash. $200-300 for multi-week trips typically sufficient.

**UK (especially London)**: Contactless payment nearly universal. Public transit, shops, restaurants all accept cards. $150-250 cash covers entire typical trip.

**Moderately card-friendly (moderate cash needed: $50-100 per day):**

**Western Europe (France, Germany, Italy, Spain)**: Cards widely accepted in cities but cash still preferred by small businesses, markets, and rural areas. Budget $60-80 daily in cash.

**Japan**: Paradoxically high-tech but cash-dominated. Credit cards accepted at major establishments but cash required at many restaurants, smaller shops, and rural areas. Plan for $80-120 daily cash.

**United States**: Cards accepted everywhere but tipping culture requires cash. Budget $40-60 daily for tips, food trucks, small vendors.

**Canada**: Very card-friendly but cash useful for markets, food stands, tips. $40-70 daily covers most needs.

**Cash-dependent destinations (substantial cash needed: $75-150 per day):**

**Southeast Asia (Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia)**: Street food, local restaurants, small shops, transportation mostly cash-only. ATMs widely available. Budget $80-120 daily cash.

**India**: Cash remains king despite digital payment growth. Many businesses cash-only, especially outside major cities. Plan for $70-100 daily.

**Morocco and North Africa**: Markets, riads, local restaurants, taxis mostly cash. Budget $90-130 daily cash.

**Central and South America (excluding Brazil)**: Cash preferred widely, card fraud concerns limit acceptance. Mexico, Peru, Colombia typically need $80-120 daily cash.

**Eastern Europe (Romania, Bulgaria, Albania)**: Growing card acceptance in cities but rural areas and smaller businesses cash-only. $60-90 daily recommended.

### Top Tourist Destinations: Specific Guidance

**Paris, France:**
Carry €200-300 ($220-330) for week-long trip. Cards accepted at hotels, major restaurants, museums. Cash needed for: small cafes, bakeries, market vendors, public toilets, tips. ATMs plentiful. Daily budget: €40-60 cash + card for major expenses.

**London, UK:**
Carry £150-200 ($195-260) maximum for entire week. Contactless cards work everywhere including public transit. Cash mainly for: tips, rare small purchases, emergencies. Daily cash needs only £15-25.

**Tokyo, Japan:**
Carry ¥30,000-50,000 ($200-335) upon arrival, replenish from ATMs. Despite being high-tech, cash dominates daily transactions. Budget ¥8,000-12,000 daily ($55-80) for meals, transit, shopping at smaller establishments. Major hotels and department stores accept cards.

**Barcelona, Spain:**
Carry €250-400 ($275-440) for week. Cards widely accepted but cash preferred at tapas bars, markets, small restaurants. Daily cash: €50-70 plus card for accommodation and major purchases.

**Rome, Italy:**
Similar to Barcelona. Budget €250-400 for week, daily €50-70 cash. Cards accepted at hotels and major attractions but small restaurants, gelato shops, and markets cash-only.

**Bangkok, Thailand:**
Carry ฿5,000-8,000 ($140-225) initial, withdraw more from ubiquitous ATMs. Street food, tuk-tuks, local restaurants, markets all cash-only. Daily budget: ฿3,000-5,000 ($85-140) cash. Hotels and malls accept cards.

**New York City, USA:**
Carry $200-400 for week. Cards accepted everywhere but cash needed extensively for: tips (hotels, restaurants, taxis, tours), food carts, some small businesses. Daily cash $40-80 depending on tipping needs.

**Dubai, UAE:**
Carry AED 500-1,000 ($135-270) for week. Extremely high card acceptance in city. Cash mainly for: tips, souks, taxis (though many now accept cards), small purchases. Daily cash AED 100-200.

**Sydney, Australia:**
Carry AUD 300-500 ($200-330) for week. Very card-friendly but cash useful for markets, small cafes, tips. Daily cash AUD 50-80 sufficient.

**Mexico City:**
Carry MXN 5,000-8,000 pesos ($290-465) for week. Cash heavily used for: street food, markets, taxis, small restaurants, tips. ATMs widely available. Daily budget: MXN 1,000-1,500 ($58-87) cash plus cards.

## How to Carry Foreign Currency Safely

Carrying substantial cash abroad creates security risks requiring strategic precautions to protect your money from theft, loss, and scams.

### Money Belt and Hidden Storage Solutions

**Money belts worn under clothing** provide the best protection for large amounts of cash, passport copies, and backup credit cards. These fabric pouches with elastic straps sit against your stomach or back under shirts, invisible to pickpockets.

Effective money belt strategies:
- **Daily walking-around money** (equivalent of $50-100): Keep in regular wallet in front pocket
- **Emergency cash and backup cards**: Store in money belt, access only in hotel room
- **Don't access money belt in public**: Reveals its existence to watching thieves

Alternative hidden storage includes:
- **Neck pouches**: Hang under shirts, good for passports and folded cash
- **Bra stashes**: Zippered pockets worn by women travelers under clothing
- **Shoe inserts**: Thin pockets in soles for emergency cash
- **Dummy wallet**: Carry cheap wallet with small amount as decoy if robbed

**Never carry cash in:**
- Back pockets (pickpocket target #1)
- Backpack outer pockets (easily accessed while wearing)
- Purse or bag not worn cross-body (snatching risk)
- Hotel rooms unsecured (use hotel safe when available)

### Daily Money Management Strategies

Minimize theft risk by carefully managing how much currency you carry daily:

**Morning routine:**
1. In hotel room (privacy), access money belt and remove day's estimated cash needs
2. Divide into two separate stashes—primary wallet and backup pocket
3. Leave majority of cash, passport, and extra cards locked in hotel safe
4. Keep emergency taxi fare ($20-30 equivalent) in separate pocket or shoe

**Throughout the day:**
- Pull out small bills only when paying, don't display wallet full of cash
- Count change carefully but away from crowds (thieves watch for carelessness)
- If you notice you're low on cash, withdraw more before completely running out
- Make mental notes of ATM locations during daily activities

**Evening routine:**
- Transfer remaining daily cash back to money belt in hotel room
- Replenish walking-around money for next day if needed
- Record approximate spending for budget tracking

**High-risk situations requiring extra caution:**
- Crowded tourist attractions, markets, and public transit (pickpocket hotspots)
- ATM withdrawals in quiet areas or at night (choose busy, well-lit locations)
- Displaying money when asking prices or negotiating (use phone calculator instead)
- Photographing currency or landmarks while holding your wallet

### Splitting Money Between Travel Companions

Couples and groups should distribute cash and cards among multiple people to protect against total loss if one person is robbed:

**Two-person travel:**
- Person A: Carries 60% of total cash, two credit cards, passport
- Person B: Carries 40% of cash, two different credit cards, passport
- Each person stores 50% of their cash in hidden money belt, 50% in accessible wallet

This ensures robbery of one person doesn't eliminate all funds. Remaining person can still access money, cards, and identification to help resolve situation.

**Family or group travel:**
- Distribute currency among 2-3 trusted adults
- Keep one credit card with parent at hotel if children going out separately
- Teach children to carry only small amounts in front pockets

### What to Do If Money Is Stolen

Despite precautions, theft sometimes occurs. Having a response plan minimizes damage:

**Immediate actions:**
1. File police report (required for insurance claims, card cancellation)
2. Call credit card companies to cancel stolen cards (keep toll-free numbers separate from cards)
3. Contact your bank to cancel debit cards and warn about potential fraud
4. Notify your embassy or consulate if passport also stolen

**Financial recovery options:**
- Use backup cards stored separately (reason to split payment methods)
- Emergency wire transfer from family or friends via Western Union or similar
- Emergency cash advance from credit card company through local bank
- Embassy loans for US citizens (must be repaid)

**Prevention lessons:**
Most theft victims had warning signs they missed. Thieves often work in teams—one distracts while another picks pockets. Stay alert in crowds, declining friendly strangers offering "help" or creating distractions.

## Currency Customs and Declaration Requirements

International travelers must comply with currency import/export regulations to avoid confiscation, fines, and legal problems at customs.

### US Currency Regulations for Travelers

**Entering or leaving the United States:**
You must declare to Customs and Border Protection (CBP) if carrying more than $10,000 in any form of currency or monetary instruments. This includes:
- US dollars (bills and coins)
- Foreign currency (all denominations combined)
- Traveler's checks
- Money orders
- Negotiable instruments

**How to declare:**
- Fill out FinCEN Form 105 (Currency Reporting Form) online before travel, or
- Request paper form from CBP officer at customs
- Include in CBP Declaration Form 6059B when entering US

**Important clarifications:**
- $10,000 limit applies to individual travelers or family groups traveling together
- Failure to declare can result in: total confiscation of currency, civil penalties up to $500,000, criminal penalties including imprisonment
- There is NO LIMIT on how much you can bring—only requirement to declare over $10,000
- Declaration doesn't mean paying taxes or fees—it's just informational reporting

**Family travel example:**
Family of four carries $12,000 total ($3,000 per person). Because they're traveling together as family unit, this exceeds $10,000 threshold and must be declared—even though no individual carries over $10,000.

### Other Countries' Currency Limits

**European Union:**
€10,000 limit for entering or leaving EU. Cash exceeding this must be declared when crossing external Schengen borders. No declaration needed for travel between EU member states.

**United Kingdom:**
£10,000 limit (or foreign currency equivalent) entering or leaving UK. Declaration required above this amount.

**Canada:**
CAD $10,000 limit. Must declare if bringing more into or taking out of Canada.

**Australia:**
AUD $10,000 limit. Physical currency and bearer negotiable instruments must be declared.

**United Arab Emirates:**
AED 60,000 limit (approximately $16,300). Amounts above must be declared.

**China:**
USD $5,000 (or equivalent) limit for foreign currency entering China. Chinese yuan (RMB) limited to ¥20,000. Special forms required for amounts above limits.

**India:**
Foreign tourists can bring unlimited foreign currency but must declare if exceeding $5,000 cash or $10,000 total including traveler's checks. Indian rupees cannot be brought into India except INR 25,000 for Indian residents.

**Thailand:**
฿500,000 limit (approximately $14,000) entering or exiting Thailand. Higher amounts require declaration.

**Japan:**
¥1,000,000 limit (approximately $6,700) or equivalent in foreign currency. Declaration required above this amount.

**Penalties for non-declaration:**
Most countries impose severe penalties for failing to declare currency exceeding limits:
- Immediate confiscation of all currency (not just excess)
- Fines ranging from 10-50% of undeclared amount
- Criminal charges possible for intentional evasion
- Travel bans or visa restrictions for future visits

**Pro tip:** Keep receipts for large currency exchanges or ATM withdrawals when traveling. If questioned about currency sources, documentation helps explain legitimate origin.

## Smart Money-Saving Strategies for International Travel

Optimize currency management to keep more money in your pocket instead of losing it to fees, bad exchange rates, and unnecessary conversions.

### Pre-Trip Currency Planning

**2-3 weeks before departure:**
Research current exchange rates using CurrencyToCurrency.app to understand true value of money in your destination. This baseline helps identify bad offers.

Compare costs of getting foreign currency through:
- Your bank (typically 3-5% markup, order 5-7 days ahead)
- Online exchange services (2-4% markup, shipping required)
- Airport exchange upon arrival (10-15% markup—avoid)
- ATMs in destination (best option: 1-3% total cost)

**Decision:** For most travelers, taking minimal pre-converted cash ($100-200) plus using destination ATMs provides best value.

**Notify banks and credit card companies:**
Alert all financial institutions about travel dates and destinations. Fraud detection systems block unexpected foreign transactions, potentially leaving you stranded. Most banks allow travel notifications through mobile apps.

**Week before departure:**
- Apply for credit card with no foreign transaction fees if you don't have one (Chase Sapphire, Capital One Venture, etc.)
- Change debit card PIN to 4 digits if currently 5-6 digits (international ATMs typically require 4-digit PINs)
- Print emergency contact numbers for all banks and card companies
- Load multi-currency travel card if using one (Wise, Revolut, etc.)

### Maximizing Value of Currency Exchanges

**Getting best exchange rates:**
Wait until arrival and use bank ATMs in your destination. This typically provides rates within 1-3% of mid-market rate—far better than pre-travel exchanges.

**ATM strategy for best rates:**
- Use ATMs at major banks (not independent machines in shops or airports)
- Withdraw larger amounts ($200-400) less frequently to minimize per-transaction fees
- Always decline "dynamic currency conversion" when offered—choose to be charged in local currency
- Avoid ATMs at airports which sometimes charge higher fees; wait until reaching city

**What is dynamic currency conversion (DCC)?**
ATMs and payment terminals sometimes ask: "Would you like to be charged in your home currency?" This sounds convenient but costs 3-7% extra through terrible exchange rates. **Always decline and choose local currency.**

**Credit card optimization:**
- Use card with no foreign transaction fees for all possible purchases (60-70% of expenses)
- These cards apply mid-market exchange rates automatically—best rates available
- Build points/miles rewards on travel spending
- Avoid cash advances on credit cards (expensive fees plus immediate interest charges)

### Avoiding Common Money-Losing Mistakes

**Mistake #1: Converting at airport exchanges**
Airport currency kiosks charge 10-15% markups, turning $1,000 into $850-900 equivalent. Even "zero commission" offers hide huge markups in poor exchange rates. Wait for destination ATMs.

**Mistake #2: Carrying too much cash from home**
Needs typically lower than anticipated because cards accepted more widely than expected. Over-conversion means paying fees twice when exchanging unused currency back.

**Mistake #3: Multiple small ATM withdrawals**
Each withdrawal costs $3-10 in fees. Making five $50 withdrawals costs $15-50 (6-20% of total). Instead, make two $125 withdrawals costing $6-20 total (2.4-4%).

**Mistake #4: Not keeping receipts**
Save ATM receipts showing amount withdrawn and exchange rate applied. If bank disputes charges or you notice errors, documentation protects you.

**Mistake #5: Paying bills in hotels/restaurants in dollars**
Some establishments offer to charge your card in USD instead of local currency. This uses their terrible exchange rate and costs 5-10% extra. Always insist on local currency charges.

**Mistake #6: Over-relying on one payment method**
Travelers with only one credit card face catastrophe if it's lost, stolen, or declined. Carry multiple cards from different issuers (Visa and Mastercard) plus cash backup.

**Mistake #7: Not researching destination-specific payment quirks**
Some countries have unique payment systems. For example, China's WeChat Pay and Alipay dominate but require Chinese bank accounts. Germany remains surprisingly cash-dependent despite being developed. Research payment norms beforehand.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Foreign Currency for Vacation

**Q: Should I exchange money before I leave or at my destination?**

For most destinations, wait until arrival and withdraw from ATMs. You'll get exchange rates 5-15% better than pre-travel exchanges at banks or airports. Exception: If visiting a country with limited ATM access, exchange a moderate amount ($200-300) before departure as safety buffer.

**Q: How do I know if I'm getting a good exchange rate?**

Check the mid-market rate on CurrencyToCurrency.app before exchanging. This is the true rate banks use when trading with each other. Any rate offered to you will be worse, but by how much? If you're getting a rate within 1-3% of mid-market, it's excellent. Within 5% is acceptable. More than 10% markup means find another option.

**Q: What's the best credit card for international travel?**

Any major travel rewards card with zero foreign transaction fees works well. Top options include Chase Sapphire Preferred (no foreign fees, 2X points on travel/dining), Capital One Venture X (no foreign fees on any Capital One card), and American Express Platinum (no foreign fees, airport lounge access). Most important feature is the zero foreign transaction fee—saves 3% on every purchase.

**Q: Can I use my debit card internationally?**

Yes, but check with your bank about foreign ATM fees and international withdrawal limits first. Most banks charge $3-5 per ATM withdrawal plus 1-3% foreign transaction fee. Some banks like Charles Schwab reimburse all ATM fees worldwide, making them excellent for international travel. Always decline dynamic currency conversion at ATMs.

**Q: What if I run out of money abroad?**

Multiple options exist: Use backup credit card stored separately, withdraw more from ATM with second debit card, request emergency wire transfer from family via Western Union or MoneyGram, use credit card cash advance at local bank (expensive but available), or contact your country's embassy for emergency loans (US citizens only, must be repaid).

**Q: Should I tip in local currency or US dollars?**

Always tip in local currency. Service workers need local currency for daily expenses and may face challenges exchanging small amounts of foreign currency. Tipping in USD creates inconvenience and may be seen as disrespectful. Check local tipping customs—some countries don't have tipping culture and it can offend.

**Q: What do I do with leftover foreign currency?**

Best strategy depends on amount. Small amounts (under $50 equivalent): spend at airport before departure on snacks, souvenirs, or duty-free purchases. Moderate amounts ($50-200): save for future trips to same destination or currency zone, give to friends traveling there soon, or donate to airport charity boxes accepting foreign coins/bills. Large amounts (over $200): exchange at your home bank or use Travelex buyback (rates poor but better than keeping unusable currency).

**Q: Do all ATMs accept US debit cards?**

Most ATMs in tourist destinations accept Visa and Mastercard/Maestro debit cards. However, some regional ATM networks only accept local cards. Look for ATMs at major international banks (HSBC, Citibank, Deutsche Bank, etc.) which reliably accept US cards. If ATM rejects your card, try different bank's ATM rather than assuming no ATMs will work.

**Q: How do I split costs when traveling in a group?**

Several apps help: Splitwise tracks who paid what and calculates who owes whom at trip end, avoiding need to split every meal check. Revolut and Wise offer group expense features. Simple solution: designate one person as "treasurer" who pays for most shared expenses (hotels, meals, activities) using their credit card, and others Venmo/Zelle their shares in USD after returning home. This minimizes currency exchange frequency.

- Skip airport currency exchange entirely

**Throughout trip:**
- Use no-foreign-fee credit card for 60-70% of expenses
- Decline dynamic currency conversion at ATMs and payment terminals

The days of traveler's checks and large currency exchanges before departure have passed.
